Smoked brisket

About 10 lb, overnight brine, roughly fourteen hours on the Egg. Twelve hours of patience for twenty minutes of dinner.

Makes 12 servings. A 10 lb packer loses roughly 40% to trim and rendering.

Ingredients

  • 10 lbWhole packer brisket
  • 11 gKosher salt, per pound of trimmed weight, 10–12 gOr: Any coarse salt. Table salt at roughly half the volume — finer crystals pack tighter
  • 3 tbspCoarse black pepper
  • 2 tbspPaprika
  • 1½ tbspGarlic powder
  • 1½ tbspOnion powder
  • 1 tbspChili powderOr: Not interchangeable with cayenne — American chili powder is a mild blend, cayenne is far hotter
  • 1 tspCumin
  • 1 tbspBrown sugar, optional
  • 3 tbspYellow mustard, binder onlyOr: Hot sauce, oil, or nothing — the binder cooks off entirely and contributes no flavour
  • 2 tbspBeef tallow, optional, at the wrap

Equipment

  • Big Green Egg or other ceramic smokerAnything that holds 250F for twelve hours with clean smoke.Or: Offset smoker; Kettle grill with a two-zone snake; Pellet smoker
  • Conveggtor (ceramic heat deflector)Direct heat under a brisket for twelve hours burns the bottom long before the inside renders.Or: Any indirect setup: a drip pan between fire and meat, or a foiled pizza stone
  • Leave-in probe thermometerNo substituteThis cook is gated by internal temperature for its entire length. An instant-read means opening the dome every twenty minutes, which is how a twelve-hour cook becomes a sixteen-hour cook.
  • Butcher paperOr: Heavy foil, at a cost — foil traps steam and softens the bark you spent eight hours building
  • Cooler and two towelsThe long hold is part of the cook, not storage.Or: Oven set to its lowest setting; Any insulated box

Temperatures

  • Whole brisket, at the wrapPull at 165 °FRange 160–170F, but bark condition decides, not the number.
  • Whole brisket, finishPull at 203 °F, no higher than 207 °FCommonly 195–207F. Probe feel is the real endpoint; 203 is where it often lands, not a rule.

Steps

  1. 1

    Trim the fat cap to about ¼ inch

    For: The whole packer

    Time: 20 min

    Take off hard, waxy fat and square up loose edges. Leave about ¼ inch of cap.

    Not needed

    This is the opposite of the steak rule. On a four-minute steak you leave the cap alone because it cannot render in time. Over twelve hours it renders completely and bastes the meat on the way — but more than about ¼ inch never fully renders and comes off the plate as a greasy layer.

  2. 2

    Dry brine overnight

    For: The whole packer, bare — no rub yet

    Time: 10 min · 8 hours is the floor, 12–16 is better, 24 is fine.

      10–12 g kosher salt per pound of trimmed weight, every surface, uncovered on a rack in the fridge.

    • 3

      Binder and rub

      For: The whole packer

      Time: 15 min

      Thin coat of mustard, then the rub over every surface.

      Not needed

      No extra salt in the rub. The brine already did it, and the two stacking is the most common way a brisket comes out inedible.

      The mustard is not a flavour — it cooks off entirely. It is glue. Hot sauce, oil or nothing all work the same.

    • 4

      Light the fire and set up indirect

      For: Nothing moves yet — the brisket stays in the fridge

      Go by sightClean coals, thin blue or invisible smoke, no white acrid smoke.Time estimate: 55 min

      • Lid closed
      • Vents bottom open about an inch, top mostly closed
      • Zone indirect
      • Temp 255 °F

      Clean the ash out first — ash over the bottom vent is the most common reason a ceramic will not hold temperature. Conveggtor in. Settle at 250–260F before anything goes on, and let the fire burn clean. Add modest oak or hickory.

      Not needed

      Resist loading the firebox to the brim. A fully lit bed is harder to hold down at 250 than a conservative one is to bring up, and an over-ignited bed is exactly how this cook spiked to 390F.

    • 5

      Brisket on, fat cap toward the hotter side

      For: The whole packer

      Time: 5 min

      • Lid closed
      • Zone indirect
      • Temp 255 °F

      Probe into the thickest part of the flat, not the point.

    • 6

      Hold 250–260F until the bark sets

      For: The brisket, undisturbed

      When the probe reads 165 °FTime estimate: 7 h · Roughly 7 hours, but this is an estimate only. The stall can add hours and it is normal. Go by the probe.

      • Lid closed
      • Vents small adjustments only
      • Zone indirect
      • Temp 255 °F

      Leave it alone. Vent changes take 5–10 minutes to register on a ceramic, so adjust once and wait rather than chasing the needle.

      Not needed

      Do not panic at a temperature spike. On the recorded cook the dome hit about 390F for five minutes when the charcoal caught more broadly than expected, came back down through 370, 320 and 295, and the brisket still finished tender at 203F. A brief overshoot on a twelve-hour cook is noise, not a ruined brisket.

    • 7

      Wrap — but only once the bark is right

      For: The brisket

      Go by sightBark is dark, firm and mahogany, and does not smear when you touch it. Internal is usually 160–170F when this is true.Time estimate: 15 min

      • Lid closed
      • Zone indirect
      • Temp 255 °F

      Butcher paper, tight. Optional 2–3 tbsp tallow. Back on, probe back in.

      Not needed

      The temperature is a signal, not the instruction. Wrapping a brisket whose bark has not set because the probe said 165 gives you a soft, steamed exterior that no amount of later time recovers. If the bark is not ready, wait.

      Foil works and softens the bark. That is a real trade, not a mistake — just know which one you are choosing.

    • 8

      Finish to probe-tender

      For: The brisket, wrapped

      Go by feelA probe slides into the thickest part of the flat with no resistance, like room-temperature butter. Internal is commonly 195–207F when this happens.Time estimate: 3 h · Three hours is typical and it varies by hours between briskets.

      • Lid closed
      • Zone indirect
      • Temp 255 °F

      On the recorded cook the lowest reading at finish was 203F.

      Not needed

      Do not pull at a number. 203F is where briskets often become tender, not why they become tender — collagen conversion is a time-at-temperature process and two briskets of the same weight can finish two hours apart. The probe tells you; the thermometer only estimates.

    • 9

      Vent the wrap 5–10 minutes

      For: The brisket, wrap opened

      Time: 10 min

      Open the wrap and let the steam off before the long hold. Skipping this carries on cooking it inside its own packet.

    • 10

      Rewrap loosely and hold 1–3 hours

      For: The brisket

      Time: 2 h · This is your buffer. Stretch or shrink it to hit your serving time.

      Loosely rewrapped, in a cooler under two towels.

      Not needed

      There will be a lot of liquid in the wrap. That is normal — save it separately for slicing or for beans. Do not pour it back over the bark, which undoes the bark.

    • 11

      Separate the point, slice the flat across the grain

      For: The whole brisket

      Time: 20 min

      The flat and the point have grain running in different directions, so find the fat seam, separate them, and slice each against its own grain. Flat at about ¼ inch; point thicker, or cube it for burnt ends.

    Rest

    Whole brisket, wrapped1 h – 3 hWrapped, in a cooler under towels

    Unlike a steak, this rest is part of the cook — collagen keeps converting and the muscle reabsorbs liquid. It is also the single most useful buffer in the recipe: if the cook runs long, shorten the hold; if it runs short, extend it. Plan to finish early on purpose.
